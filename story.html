Overview

What you are
actually drinking

Most tea is an infusion: you steep leaves, then throw them away. Matcha is not. The whole leaf is stone-ground into powder and whisked into water, so you swallow the leaf itself. That is why one bowl tastes so much fuller than a teabag, and why the quality of the leaf has nowhere to hide.

The plants are covered for roughly three to four weeks before picking. Kept out of direct sun, the leaf makes more chlorophyll and more of the amino acid that gives matcha its savoury, almost sweet depth — and less of the bitterness that heavy sunlight builds up. That shading is the whole difference between matcha and ordinary green tea.

After picking, the leaves are steamed within hours, dried, and stripped of every stem and vein. What is left is called tencha, and only tencha becomes matcha. Granite wheels then turn slowly enough to grind about 30 to 40 grams an hour — a full tin takes most of a working day.

    Tabi · The journey

    Hillside
    to Kuwait

    1. Shaded

      Reed screens go over the field three to four weeks before picking.

    2. Picked

      Only the first harvest, in the spring, and only the youngest leaves.

    3. Steamed

      Within hours, to stop oxidation and lock the colour in.

    4. De-stemmed

      Stems and veins are blown away. What is left is tencha.

    5. Stone-milled

      Granite wheels, turning slowly so the powder never heats up.

    6. Sealed & flown

      Nitrogen-flushed tins, air-freighted, and kept cold in our Kuwait store.

    Yakusoku · Our promise

    Small tins,
    bought often

    Matcha fades. Light, heat and air pull the green out of it within weeks of opening. So we buy in small lots, stamp every tin with the month it was ground, and would rather run out than sell you something tired.
